Introduction

The original Game Boy makes sound without ever handling a sound. There is no sample buffer, no mixer and no sound card. The processor is far too slow to work out a waveform one sample at a time: at CD quality that would be 44,100 numbers a second, and nothing would be left over for the game. Instead the console has four small synthesiser circuits, called channels, built into the chip. Once the program has said what kind of wave, how high, how loud and for how long, a channel makes its sound entirely on its own, and goes on making it while the processor does something else.

That changes how you think about sound. On most machines you produce the signal. On the Game Boy you describe it: “this note, that shape, fade out over a second”, and the hardware plays it. What is left for the program is timing: starting the right note at the right moment, sixty times a second. This book teaches sound the way the machine wants it taught: ten topics, each starting with the job you want done and showing the Game Boy's own way of doing it, and a last chapter that adds the buttons and turns the console into an instrument. Graphics have their own companion book, Pixels, Tiles & Sprites, and the link cable and cartridge hardware are not covered.

What you should know first

This book assumes you can read basic Game Boy (SM83) assembly: registers A, B, C, D, E, H, L, the 16-bit pairs such as HL, LD, ADD, CP, the flags, JR and CALL. If you can follow a loop that counts from 1 to 10, you are ready. Everything to do with sound is explained from scratch, and you need no music theory beyond knowing that a higher note is a faster vibration.

The four channels

Every sound the Game Boy makes is the sum of four channels, each a different kind of instrument:

ChannelKindWhat it is good for
1Square wave, with a frequency sweepMelodies, and effects that slide: jumps, lasers
2Square waveHarmony, arpeggios, short blips
3A wave you draw yourself: 32 samplesBass, and unusual tones
4NoiseDrums, explosions, wind

A square wave is the simplest sound there is: the speaker is pushed out, held, pulled back, held, and so on, so the signal is either high or low and nothing in between. It is the buzzy sound of the Game Boy that everyone knows. Each channel has its own handful of registers, and each can be sent to the left speaker, the right, or both.

The sound memory map

As with the screen, all of it is controlled by writing to memory addresses. The sound hardware lives from $FF10 to $FF3F:

AddressWhat it holds
$FF10-$FF14Channel 1: sweep, duty and length, envelope, pitch and start
$FF16-$FF19Channel 2: the same, without a sweep
$FF1A-$FF1EChannel 3: on/off, length, volume, pitch and start
$FF20-$FF23Channel 4: length, envelope, noise setting and start
$FF24-$FF26Master controls: volume, which channel goes to which speaker, and the on/off switch
$FF30-$FF3FWave RAM: the 32 samples that channel 3 plays

The registers are named after their address: rNR11 is the “NR” (sound) register of channel 1, number 1. Appendix B lists them all. Many sound registers are partly or entirely write-only: reading one back gives you only some of what you wrote, and the rest as ones. If you need to know a note's pitch later, keep your own copy in RAM.

Building and running the examples

The programs are assembled with RGBDS, the standard Game Boy toolchain. Every program in this book is a complete file in the examples/ folder, and every routine is in lib/. To build one:

rgbasm -I lib/ -o beep_demo.o examples/beep_demo.asm
rgblink -o beep_demo.gb beep_demo.o
rgbfix -v -p 0xFF beep_demo.gb

or simply run make beep_demo.gb in the examples folder, and make run-beep_demo to open it in an emulator. Turn the volume up. The listings you read here are not retyped: the book is built from the same files that an automated test suite assembles and runs on an emulated Game Boy, recording the sound it makes and checking its pitch, its shape and its timing against calculations of what it should be.

The shape of every program

Every example begins with the same few lines, kept in boot.inc:

; boot.inc - the first lines of every program in this book.
; Puts the cartridge header space at $100 and starts your code at $150
; with the stack at the top of the first block of work RAM.

INCLUDE "hardware.inc"

SECTION "Header", ROM0[$100]
    NOP
    JP Start
    DS $150 - @, 0           ; rgbfix fills in the header here

SECTION "Main", ROM0[$150]
Start:
    LD SP, $CFFF             ; The stack grows down from the end of WRAM bank 0

The Game Boy starts executing at $100, where there are only four bytes of room before the cartridge header, so the program jumps over it. rgbfix fills the header in afterwards. Code then starts at $150, with the stack placed at the top of the first block of work RAM. hardware.inc gives names to the hardware registers (rNR12, rNR52 and so on) and is used with LDH, the short instruction for the $FF00 page.

The routines are plain files you INCLUDE at the end of a program: sound.asm for the basics, frame.asm for keeping time, music.asm for songs, and so on. Appendix A lists them all. Sound is not something you can look at, so each example also prints a line or two of text saying what it is playing; the small screen.asm and text.asm routines do that, and you can ignore them until you want to copy them. Each chapter ends with a few exercises that use only what has been covered so far.

1. Sound on the Game Boy

[Back to Contents]

In High-Level Languages

You play a sound by asking for it by name. Somewhere underneath, a library has decoded a file into a long list of samples, numbers describing the position of the speaker at each instant, and hands them to the sound card at a fixed rate:

sound = load("beep.wav")        # thousands of samples, decoded for you
sound.play()                    # the library feeds them to the sound card

# what the sound card is really doing, 44100 times a second:
for each tick:
    speaker = sum of the next sample of every sound that is playing

The program owns every sample. To make a new sound, it computes the list; to change a sound, it recomputes it. That is simple to reason about, but it is expensive: one second of mono sound at 44,100 samples is 44,100 numbers that must be found in time, every second. The Game Boy was designed to avoid exactly that cost.

In Game Boy Assembly

The Game Boy has no sample list. It has four circuits that each generate their own wave, and a few registers to tell them what to do. A program makes a sound in three steps: switch the sound hardware on, set up a channel, and start it. After that the channel plays by itself.

Switching the sound on

There are three master registers, and they are the first things any program sets:

RegisterAddressMeaning
rNR52$FF26Bit 7: the whole sound chip on (1) or off (0). Bits 3–0, when read: which of channels 4–1 are playing right now
rNR50$FF24Master volume, 0–7, for the left speaker (bits 6–4) and the right speaker (bits 2–0)
rNR51$FF25Panning: one bit per channel per speaker. Bits 7–4 send channels 4–1 to the left, bits 3–0 send channels 4–1 to the right

The value $77 in rNR50 is full volume on both sides; $FF in rNR51 sends every channel to both. A single channel to both speakers is a pair of bits: $11 is channel 1 only, $22 channel 2 only, $44 channel 3, $88 channel 4. (The test suite uses exactly this to listen to one channel at a time.) sound_init sets all three, and starts with a little ritual:

sound_init:
    XOR A
    LDH [rNR52], A          ; Switch the sound chip off: this clears every sound register ...
    LD A, $80
    LDH [rNR52], A          ; ... and on again.  Do this before any other sound register: while it is off they ignore writes
    LD A, $77
    LDH [rNR50], A          ; Full volume, left and right
    LD A, $FF
    LDH [rNR51], A          ; Send every channel to both speakers
    RET

Writing 0 and then $80 to rNR52 switches the chip off and on again. While it is off, every other sound register ignores writes, and turning it off clears them all. So the pair is a reset: whatever state the console's start-up left the sound chip in (it has just played its own start-up chime), the program begins from silence with every register at zero. Many programs write only $80 and rely on the start-up state; this one resets because it costs two instructions and makes the result independent of the machine. It was needed in practice: in the emulator used to test this book, a channel started without the reset stayed silent. Do it before touching anything else.

Starting a note

Channel 1 has five registers. Four of them describe the note:

RegisterAddressMeaning
rNR11$FF11Bits 7–6: the shape of the square wave (the duty, Chapter 3). Bits 5–0: length (Chapter 5)
rNR12$FF12The volume envelope: how loud, and whether it fades (Chapter 4)
rNR13$FF13Pitch, low 8 bits
rNR14$FF14Bit 7: start the sound. Bit 6: stop when the length runs out. Bits 2–0: pitch, high 3 bits

The important one is bit 7 of rNR14. A channel does not start when you set its registers; it starts when you write a 1 to the start bit (the hardware calls it the trigger). Everything you want must therefore be written first, and the write to NR14 goes last. Writing it again starts the note again from the beginning, which is how a program plays one note after another.

A complete example

This program switches the sound on, prints a line, and plays channel 1 at about 2048 Hz, a high, thin beep, with a volume that starts at the maximum and fades out over about a second. When it has started the note it has nothing left to do:

; beep_demo.asm - Chapter 1: switch the sound on and play one note
; Channel 1 plays a square wave at 2048 Hz for about a second, fading out.
INCLUDE "boot.inc"

    CALL screen_init
    LD B, 2
    LD C, 2
    LD HL, Title
    CALL show_text

    CALL sound_init

    LD A, DUTY_50
    LDH [rNR11], A              ; 50% square wave
    LD A, $F3
    LDH [rNR12], A              ; start at full volume, fade out slowly
    LD A, $C0
    LDH [rNR13], A              ; pitch, low 8 bits  ($7C0 = 1984)
    LD A, NRX4_START | $07
    LDH [rNR14], A              ; start the sound; the 3 low bits are the top of the pitch

Done:
    HALT                        ; the sound plays by itself: nothing left to do
    NOP
    JR Done

Title: DB "Channel 1: 2048 Hz", 0

INCLUDE "gfx.asm"
INCLUDE "text.asm"
INCLUDE "screen.asm"
INCLUDE "sound.asm"

What you hear: a single beep that fades away in about a second, and then silence. The program stopped at Done long before the sound did: HALT puts the processor to sleep, and the sound chip carries on regardless. The test suite records the sound the emulated chip produces and measures it: the pitch measures 2046 Hz (the value $7C0 gives exactly 131072 / 64 = 2048 Hz; the measurement is good to a few hertz), the wave is high exactly half the time, the loudness falls, and a second later the output is silent again.

Two of the values are worth decoding now, and Chapters 2 and 4 return to them. $F3 in rNR12 means “start at volume 15, and fade”; $7C0 is the pitch, split between rNR13 ($C0) and the low three bits of rNR14 ($07), exactly as the program writes it.

Key fact: a channel plays by itself. Set it up, write the start bit last, and the program is free; sound is the one thing the Game Boy does that needs no help from the processor once begun.

Try it yourself

  1. Change $C0 to $00. Is the beep higher or lower? Change the $07 to $03. Why does that one change make a much larger difference?
  2. Change rNR50 in sound_init to $11. What changes, and why is it not silent?
  3. Change rNR51 to $10 so that channel 1 only reaches the left speaker. With headphones, which ear plays it?

2. Pitch & Notes

[Back to Contents]

In High-Level Languages

You name a note and the library finds the frequency, using floating point and a power of two. A semitone is the twelfth root of two:

frequency = 440 * 2 ** ((note - 69) / 12)       # MIDI numbering: A4 = 69
osc.frequency = frequency                       # any real number will do

Any real number will do, and the oscillator turns it into a wave without you ever seeing the arithmetic. That is exactly what a Game Boy has no room for: no floating point, no powers, not even a multiply instruction.

In Game Boy Assembly

A square channel does not take a frequency. It takes an 11-bit number, x, from 0 to 2047, and counts: the larger the number, the shorter the wait between flips of the wave, and the higher the note. The frequency you hear is

frequency = 131072 / (2048 - x)  Hz

so the lowest possible note (x = 0) is 64 Hz and the highest is more than 100,000 Hz, far above anything you can hear. Everything musical happens in the top fraction of the range, where x is close to 2047.

A note, worked by hand

The note A above middle C is 440 Hz. Rearranging the formula for x:

x = 2048 - 131072 / 440
  = 2048 - 297.9
  = 1750.1   which rounds to 1750 = $6D6

The 11 bits are written in two pieces: the low 8 bits ($D6) go in rNR13, and the top 3 bits ($6 is %110) go in the low three bits of rNR14, with the start bit set: $86. The note then sounds at 131072 / (2048 − 1750) = 439.8 Hz, about two-thirds of a cent flat of 440.

The note table

A program needs hundreds of different notes and cannot do that division on the machine, so the work is done once, in advance. NoteTable in sound.asm holds the 11-bit value for 72 notes, six octaves from C2 (65 Hz) to B7 (3951 Hz), two bytes each. The notes are numbered from 0, and every semitone adds one, so the number of a note is a position in the table:

; C2 is note 0, and every semitone adds one: C3 = 12, C4 = 24, A4 = 33 (440 Hz).
; A sharp is written As, so As3 is the note a semitone above A3.

DEF C2 EQU 0

notes.inc gives every note a name: C2, Cs2 for C sharp, D2 and so on up to B7, so a program can say LD A, A4. Finding a note in the table is a doubling (two bytes each) and an add:

; --- DE = the pitch value of note A ---
note_freq:
    LD L, A
    LD H, 0
    ADD HL, HL              ; two bytes per note
    LD DE, NoteTable
    ADD HL, DE
    LD A, [HL+]
    LD E, A
    LD D, [HL]
    RET

The values in the table are the formula above, rounded to a whole number, for a tuning of A4 = 440 Hz. Rounding costs something, and it costs most at the top. In the lowest octaves one step of x is a tiny fraction of a note; by B7 a step is about half a semitone, so the highest notes can be up to a fifth of a semitone out of tune. The test suite checks every entry against the formula: in the lowest four octaves each is within 6 cents of true, in the sixth within 11 and in the top octave within 20, where a cent is a hundredth of a semitone.

Starting a note on channel 1, 2 or 3 is then three lines, because the three channels have the same layout, just at different addresses: the low byte to NRx3 and the high bits, with the start flag, to NRx4:

; --- Start a note: low 8 bits to NRx3, then the high 3 bits with the start flag to NRx4 ---
play_ch1:
    LD A, E
    LDH [rNR13], A
    LD A, D
    OR NRX4_START
    LDH [rNR14], A
    RET

play_ch2:
    LD A, E
    LDH [rNR23], A
    LD A, D
    OR NRX4_START
    LDH [rNR24], A
    RET

play_ch3:
    LD A, E
    LDH [rNR33], A
    LD A, D
    OR NRX4_START
    LDH [rNR34], A
    RET

A complete example

This program plays the C major scale, one note every 24 frames, and prints each note's name. It uses print_note, which turns a note number into a name by finding the letter in a table and counting octaves with repeated subtraction of 12:

; scale_demo.asm - Chapter 2: notes from a table
; Plays the C major scale on channel 1, one note every 24 frames, and prints the
; name of each note.  The pitch values come from NoteTable in sound.asm.
SECTION "ScaleVars", WRAM0
wIndex: DS 1                    ; which note of the scale is next

INCLUDE "boot.inc"
INCLUDE "notes.inc"

    CALL screen_init
    CALL sound_init
    LD B, 2
    LD C, 2
    LD HL, Title
    CALL show_text

    LD A, DUTY_50
    LDH [rNR11], A
    XOR A
    LD [wIndex], A
    CALL frame_init
    EI

NextNote:
    LD A, [wIndex]
    LD E, A
    LD D, 0
    LD HL, Scale
    ADD HL, DE
    LD A, [HL]                  ; A = the note number
    PUSH AF
    LD B, 2
    LD C, 5
    CALL print_at
    POP AF
    PUSH AF
    CALL print_note             ; show its name
    POP AF
    CALL note_freq              ; DE = its pitch value
    LD A, $A2
    LDH [rNR12], A              ; volume 10, fading by one step every 2/64 s
    CALL play_ch1

    LD B, 24
.wait:
    CALL wait_frame
    DEC B
    JR NZ, .wait

    LD A, [wIndex]
    INC A
    CP 8
    JR NZ, .store
    XOR A                       ; after the eighth note, start the scale again
.store:
    LD [wIndex], A
    JR NextNote

Title: DB "C major scale", 0
Scale: DB C4, D4, E4, F4, G4, A4, B4, C5

INCLUDE "gfx.asm"
INCLUDE "text.asm"
INCLUDE "screen.asm"
INCLUDE "frame.asm"
INCLUDE "sound.asm"

What you hear: eight notes going up, the usual do-re-mi, ending on the C an octave above where it started. The tests measure each one from the recorded sound and compare it to 440 × 2(n−33)/12; all are within 15 cents. Look at what the loop does not do: it never counts a wave or a sample. It looks up a number, writes it, and waits.

Key fact: pitch is an 11-bit number, 131072 / (2048 − x) Hz, written in two pieces. A table in ROM replaces the arithmetic, and the start bit in NRx4 goes last.

3. Square Waves & Duty

[Back to Contents]

In High-Level Languages

An oscillator comes with a choice of shapes: sine, triangle, sawtooth, square. The shape is the timbre, what makes a flute sound unlike a violin playing the same note. A software synthesiser evaluates the chosen formula for every sample:

osc = Oscillator(type = "square", frequency = 440)
osc.pulse_width = 0.25                  # some libraries let you change the shape of the square

Changing the shape is a change of one number, and the cost is a different calculation for each of the thousands of samples per second.

In Game Boy Assembly

Channels 1 and 2 can make only one kind of wave, the square wave, but they can change how long it stays high. The fraction of each cycle that the wave is high is its duty cycle, and there are four choices, set by the top two bits of rNR11 (channel 1) or rNR21 (channel 2):

Bits 7–6Name in hardware.incHigh forSounds like
%00DUTY_121/8 of the cycleThin, reedy, a little nasal
%01DUTY_252/8Full but bright: the classic lead
%10DUTY_504/8Hollow, like a clarinet
%11DUTY_756/8Sounds the same as 25%

The last row is not a mistake. Turning a wave upside down does not change how it sounds, and a wave that is high for three quarters of the time is a wave that is low for a quarter of the time, turned upside down. So there are really three timbres to choose from, not four.

Why the shape changes the sound

The note's pitch depends only on how often the wave repeats. Its character depends on the shape within each repeat, and a thin pulse and a fat one repeat equally often. Here is the same note, A4, drawn from the actual output of channel 1 playing each duty in turn:

A 50% wave contains only the odd harmonics of its pitch (the extra tones at three, five, seven times the frequency, and so on), which is what gives it a hollow sound. A thin pulse contains many more of them, relatively stronger, and sounds brighter and more nasal. The duty cycle is the one real instrument change a square channel has, and switching it from one note to the next gives a melody a little variety.

Channel 2 has the same two bytes at different addresses, rNR21 ($FF16) and rNR22 ($FF17), then rNR23 and rNR24. The registers are in the same order as channel 1's, but with no sweep register in front, which is why channel 2 starts at $FF16 rather than $FF15: that address is unused.

A complete example

This program plays A4 four times on channel 1, once with each duty, and prints the setting. It changes only rNR11 between the notes; the pitch and envelope are written each time.

; duty_demo.asm - Chapter 3: the duty cycle of a square wave
; The same note (A4, 440 Hz) is played four times on channel 1, once with each duty
; cycle.  The pitch never changes: only the character of the sound does.
SECTION "DutyVars", WRAM0
wIndex: DS 1

INCLUDE "boot.inc"
INCLUDE "notes.inc"

    CALL screen_init
    CALL sound_init
    LD B, 2
    LD C, 2
    LD HL, Title
    CALL show_text
    XOR A
    LD [wIndex], A
    CALL frame_init
    EI

NextDuty:
    LD A, [wIndex]
    LD E, A
    LD D, 0
    LD HL, Duties
    ADD HL, DE
    LD A, [HL]
    LDH [rNR11], A              ; the duty cycle is the top two bits of NR11
    LD A, $C0
    LDH [rNR12], A              ; volume 12, steady
    LD A, A4
    CALL note_freq
    CALL play_ch1

    LD A, [wIndex]              ; the label: six characters each, so the address is label + 6 * index
    LD L, A
    LD H, 0
    LD D, H
    LD E, L
    ADD HL, HL                  ; 2 * index
    ADD HL, DE                  ; 3 * index
    ADD HL, HL                  ; 6 * index
    LD DE, Labels
    ADD HL, DE
    LD B, 2
    LD C, 5
    PUSH HL
    CALL print_at
    POP HL
    CALL print_string

    LD B, 45
.wait:
    CALL wait_frame
    DEC B
    JR NZ, .wait

    LD A, [wIndex]
    INC A
    AND 3                       ; 0, 1, 2, 3, 0, 1, ...
    LD [wIndex], A

Title: DB "Duty cycle", 0
Duties: DB DUTY_12, DUTY_25, DUTY_50, DUTY_75

What you hear: the same note, four times, changing from thin to full to hollow and back to the sound of the second. The tests record each note and measure it. The pitch is 440 Hz every time, and the wave is high for 12.5%, 25%, 50% and 75% of each cycle, to within two percent.

Notice how the label is found: the four strings are the same length (five characters and a zero), so the address of string n is the address of the first plus six times n. The program works out ×6 as (2n + n) × 2 with two additions and a doubling. Tables of equal-sized entries are the usual way to avoid any search.

Key fact: a square channel's timbre is its duty cycle, two bits of NRx1. It never changes the pitch, and 75% sounds the same as 25%.

4. Volume Envelopes

[Back to Contents]

In High-Level Languages

A note that starts and stops at full volume sounds like a switch. Real instruments have a shape in time: a piano is loudest at the start and dies away, an organ swells and holds. Audio libraries describe it as an envelope, a gain that changes while the note plays, often attack, decay, sustain and release:

for each sample at time t:
    out = wave(t) * gain(t)         # gain(t) goes 1.0 -> 0.0 over the note

In Game Boy Assembly

Channels 1, 2 and 4 each carry a small envelope generator. You do not give it a curve; you give it three numbers and it steps the volume up or down by itself. They share one register, rNRx2 (rNR12, rNR22, rNR42):

bits 7-4   bit 3        bits 2-0
volume     direction    step time
0 to 15    1 = rising   0 = none, else n/64 second per step
           0 = falling

The channel starts at the volume in the top four bits. Every step time sixty-fourths of a second it moves one level in the chosen direction, and stops when it reaches 0 or 15. So the length of a fade is volume × step time sixty-fourths of a second. Four examples, all worked out:

ValueMeaningTakes
$F1Start at 15, fall, one level every 1/64 s15 × 1/64 = 0.23 s: a pluck
$F7Start at 15, fall, one level every 7/64 s15 × 7/64 = 1.6 s: a long fade
$0BStart at 0, rise, one level every 3/64 s15 × 3/64 = 0.7 s: a swell, then it holds
$C0Start at 12, no envelope (step time 0)Never changes: a steady tone

The envelope is read when the note starts, so set it before the trigger. A step time of zero switches the envelope off and leaves the volume where it began. That is all the control there is: no attack-decay-sustain curve, only a straight line up or down at the speed you pick. Even so, careful use of it gives a surprising range:

The silent-channel rule

There is one trap. A channel's output goes through a small converter (the DAC) that is switched on by the envelope register, and it counts as on only if the top five bits of NRx2 are not all zero: any volume above zero, or the direction bit set. A value like $00 or $07 (start at zero, falling) switches the whole channel off. The swell above is $0B, not $03, for exactly this reason: with the rising bit set, it counts as on even though it starts at silence. Writing $00 to a playing channel silences it at once, but it also switches the output off, and on a real console that can make a small click as the level steps. A quieter way is to write $08 (volume 0, rising, no step time, output still on) and then start the channel again: since the envelope is read only when a note starts, the register alone changes nothing until then. Chapter 10 does this for rests.

5. Length & Sound Effects

[Back to Contents]

In High-Level Languages

A sound effect is a clip, and a clip has a length because the data runs out: the file holds 94 milliseconds of samples, so the sound lasts 94 milliseconds. You never say “stop”. You say play(coin) and the library drops the sound when the samples are used up.

play(coin)          # the clip is 0.1 s long, so it ends by itself

In Game Boy Assembly

A channel has no clip. A note keeps sounding for as long as the channel is on, even at volume zero; to end it you must either switch the channel off or stop it from the program. For short sounds there is a better answer: a length counter in each channel that counts down while the note plays and switches the channel off when it reaches zero.

It takes two things. The length itself goes in the low six bits of NRx1 (the low eight bits of rNR31 for channel 3). And the counter is only used if you ask for it, with bit 6 of NRx4:

duration = (64 - length) / 256 second       channels 1, 2 and 4   (length 0 to 63)
duration = (256 - length) / 256 second      channel 3             (length 0 to 255)

The counter runs at 256 steps a second, so the longest sound a square or noise channel can make by itself is a quarter of a second (length 0), and the shortest a 256th of a second. A length of 52 on channel 4 is (64 − 52) / 256, or 47 ms. If the length-enable bit is not set the counter is ignored, and the note plays until the envelope or the program stops it.

Building an effect

A sound effect, then, is a handful of the choices you have met: a pitch (Chapter 2), a duty (3), an envelope (4) and a length. Three small ones:

  • Blip: a short high square note. Channel 1, 25% duty, C6, full volume with no fade, length 56 (31 ms). A button press or menu cursor.
  • Coin: two notes, B5 then E6, a fourth apart. The first is cut short by its length (94 ms), the second (156 ms) fades. The second note starts six frames after the first, so the program needs a timer.
  • Bonk: a 47 ms burst of low noise on channel 4, so that it does not disturb any music on the other channels.

The coin shows the one new idea: doing something later. The program has no sleep, so it stores a countdown of frames in memory and tests it once per frame:

MainLoop:
    CALL wait_frame
    CALL read_joypad

    LD A, [wCoin]               ; is the second note of a coin due?
    OR A
    JR Z, .no_coin
    DEC A
    LD [wCoin], A
    CALL Z, coin_second
.no_coin:
    LD A, [wJoyNew]
    AND PADF_A
    CALL NZ, coin
    LD A, [wJoyNew]
    AND PADF_B
    CALL NZ, blip
    LD A, [wJoyNew]
    AND PADF_START
    CALL NZ, bonk
    JR MainLoop

When the A button goes down, coin plays the first note and sets wCoin to 6. Each frame the countdown goes down by one, and when it reaches zero CALL Z, coin_second plays the second. The program is never waiting; it is running a loop sixty times a second and each pass takes one step. Chapter 9 makes the idea systematic.

6. Frequency Sweep

[Back to Contents]

In High-Level Languages

To slide a pitch you change the frequency over time, one small step per update. Libraries offer a ramp, but under it is the same loop: every few milliseconds compute a new frequency and hand it to the oscillator.

for each update:
    osc.frequency = osc.frequency * 1.02          # up a little

That is a job for the program, repeated many times a second, for as long as the slide lasts.

In Game Boy Assembly

Channel 1 has a sweep unit that does this slide in hardware. The program starts the note and the sweep register rNR10 ($FF10) says how the pitch should move afterwards:

bit 7     bits 6-4      bit 3          bits 2-0
(unused)  step time     direction      shift
          0 = no sweep  0 = rising     0 to 7
          else n/128 s  1 = falling

Each step, the 11-bit pitch value x from Chapter 2 is changed by a fraction of itself:

rising:   x = x + x / 2^shift
falling:  x = x - x / 2^shift

so a shift of 5 means each step moves the value by 1/32, and a shift of 1 by a half. The step time is in 128ths of a second, so 1 means a step every 7.8 ms and 7 every 55 ms. A step time of 0 turns the sweep off. Three effects, all done in this way, as the example plays them:

EffectNR10DecodedStart pitch
Jump$16Step every 7.8 ms, rising, shift 6: each step adds 1/64$500 (170 Hz)
Laser$2DStep every 15.6 ms, falling, shift 5: each step takes off 1/32$7A0 (1365 Hz)
Slide$7FStep every 55 ms, falling, shift 7: each step takes off 1/128$7C0 (2048 Hz)

The sweep happens to the 11-bit value, not to the frequency you hear, and the relation between them (131072 / (2048 − x)) is not a straight line. A rising sweep therefore starts slowly and ends in a rush; a falling one dives and then lingers. The figure shows the three effects as the emulated chip played them:

One more rule makes a good sound effect for nothing. If a rising sweep tries to take the value above 2047, the hardware gives up and switches the channel off. That is how the jump ends: it climbs and then simply stops, without any envelope or length. A falling sweep cannot do this; it comes to rest near the bottom of the range and goes on playing until the envelope finishes it, which is why the laser and slide use envelopes of about the right duration.

7. The Wave Channel

[Back to Contents]

In High-Level Languages

When a library offers a custom oscillator, you give it a list of numbers describing one cycle of the wave, and it plays the list over and over at the right speed. This is wavetable synthesis:

table = [sin(2 * pi * i / 32) for i in range(32)]      # one cycle, 32 numbers
osc = WavetableOscillator(table, frequency = 220)

In Game Boy Assembly

Channel 3 is exactly that. Instead of a fixed square wave it plays a table you provide: 32 samples of 4 bits each, kept in 16 bytes of wave RAM at $FF30-$FF3F. Each sample is a level from 0 to 15, two to a byte, with the first sample in the high four bits. The channel reads the 32 samples in order, then starts again, and the faster it does so the higher the note.

Drawing a wave by hand

A sine wave is smooth, and four bits give only 16 levels, so a sine here is a staircase. The table is a recipe: sample i is 7.5 + 7.5 × sin(2π(i + 0.5) / 32), rounded. The first few, worked out:

i = 0:  sin(2pi * 0.5 / 32) = 0.10   ->   7.5 + 0.7  = 8.2   -> 8
i = 1:  sin(2pi * 1.5 / 32) = 0.29   ->   7.5 + 2.2  = 9.7   -> 10  ($A)
i = 2:  sin(2pi * 2.5 / 32) = 0.47   ->   7.5 + 3.5  = 11.0  -> 11  ($B)
i = 3:  sin(2pi * 3.5 / 32) = 0.63   ->   7.5 + 4.8  = 12.3  -> 12  ($C)

first two bytes:  $8A, $BC       (samples 0 and 1, then samples 2 and 3)

The other three shapes are even easier: a triangle counts up 0 to 15 and back down, a sawtooth counts up 0 to 15 two samples at a time and drops, and a pulse is 15 for the first eight samples and 0 for the rest. The tables are in the example below. The figure shows what each one sounded like through the emulated chip, both the staircase and the sharp edges:

The channel's registers

RegisterAddressMeaning
rNR30$FF1ABit 7: the channel on (1) or off (0)
rNR31$FF1BLength, 0–255 (Chapter 5)
rNR32$FF1CVolume, in bits 6–5: %00 silent, %01 full, %10 half, %11 a quarter
rNR33, rNR34$FF1D, $FF1EPitch and start, as for channels 1 and 2

Three differences from the square channels matter. First, there is no envelope: the volume is one of four fixed levels (silent, full, half, a quarter), chosen in NR32, and a note neither fades nor swells unless the program changes NR32 in step with the frame. Second, the channel must be off to change its wave: wave_load switches channel 3 off, copies the 16 bytes, and switches it on again. Third, the pitch is one octave lower. The channel plays 32 samples per cycle, so its frequency is

frequency = 65536 / (2048 - x)  Hz        (channels 1 and 2: 131072 / (2048 - x))

The same value x therefore gives a note an octave lower than on channel 1 or 2. The simple answer is to ask for the note an octave up: the example plays A3 by passing A3 + 12 to note_freq. (This is also why channel 3 is the usual choice for bass: its range reaches an octave lower.)

; --- Copy 16 bytes (32 samples of 4 bits) from HL into the wave RAM ---
; Channel 3 is switched off while the wave RAM is rewritten, then back on.
wave_load:
    XOR A
    LDH [rNR30], A          ; off
    LD DE, rWAVE
    LD B, 16
.copy:
    LD A, [HL+]
    LD [DE], A
    INC DE
    DEC B
    JR NZ, .copy
    LD A, WAVE_ON
    LDH [rNR30], A          ; on
    RET

8. The Noise Channel

[Back to Contents]

In High-Level Languages

Noise is just random numbers: fill a buffer with them and play it. A drum or an explosion is noise with a volume envelope, perhaps filtered:

for each sample:
    out = random() * gain(t)          # hiss

In Game Boy Assembly

Channel 4 makes noise with a linear feedback shift register, a 15-bit pattern of bits that is shifted along at a steady rate; each time it moves, a new bit is fed in, made from two of the old ones, in a way that makes the sequence look random and take 32,767 steps to repeat. The channel's output is one bit of it. The program controls two things: how fast the register is clocked, and how long the pattern is.

Both are in rNR43 ($FF22):

bits 7-4   bit 3        bits 2-0
shift s    width        divisor r
0 to 13    0 = 15 bits  0 to 7  (0 counts as 0.5)
           1 = 7 bits

clock = 262144 / (r * 2^s)  Hz

A high clock rate gives a high, hissy noise; a low one a rumble. The width bit shortens the pattern from 32,767 steps to 127. A pattern of 127 steps comes round about 258 times as often, and a repeat that fast is heard as a pitch: such a noise has a buzzing tone, at the clock rate divided by 127.

Channel 4 has an envelope (rNR42, $FF21, as in Chapter 4), a length (rNR41, Chapter 5) and a start bit in rNR44 ($FF23), but no pitch registers. Five sounds, each written as an envelope byte and a noise byte:

SoundNR42NR43Why
Kick$F2$63Shift 6, divisor 3: clock 1365 Hz, a low rumble; falls in 0.5 s
Snare$C1$33Shift 3, divisor 3: clock 10.9 kHz, a mid crack, fading in 0.2 s
Hi-hat$71$11Shift 1, divisor 1: clock 131 kHz, the highest hiss, fading in 0.1 s
Explosion$F7$67Shift 6, divisor 7: clock 585 Hz, and a 1.6 s fade
Buzz$F2$2BShift 2, divisor 3, width bit set: clock 21.8 kHz repeating every 127 steps: a 172 Hz rasp

The drum sounds make a useful rule of thumb. The higher the clock, the lighter the drum; the shorter the envelope, the sharper. Percussion in many a Game Boy song is these two bytes and nothing else.

9. Keeping Time

[Back to Contents]

In High-Level Languages

Time is a service. You ask for a timer and the system calls you back, or you sleep and are woken, and in between the processor does other work:

setInterval(play_next_note, 500)        # every half second
sleep(0.5)                              # or: wait half a second

Behind this is a clock chip and an operating system that keeps a list of timers. You never see how long a millisecond really is.

In Game Boy Assembly

The Game Boy has no operating system and no timer queue. What it does have is the screen, which is redrawn 59.73 times a second without fail, and which says so: at the end of each picture the LCD raises the VBlank interrupt. This is the program's clock, and the one every game uses. One tick of it is a frame, about 16.7 milliseconds, and every duration in a Game Boy program, from a note to a level, is counted in frames.

The sound hardware does not need this clock to make a note, but the program needs it to know when to start the next one. frame.asm is the whole mechanism:

; =============================================================
; frame.asm - counting frames (Chapter 9)
; =============================================================
; The sound hardware plays a note without any help, but somebody has to
; change the registers at the right moments.  The program's clock is the
; screen: once per frame (about 60 times a second) the LCD enters VBlank
; and raises an interrupt.  The handler below just notes that it happened.
;
;   frame_init    enable the VBlank interrupt (call before EI)
;   wait_frame    sleep until the next frame has started
;
; wTicks counts frames since frame_init (and wraps at 256).
; The LCD must be on, or no VBlank ever comes.

SECTION "FrameVectorTable", ROM0[$40]
    JP VBlankHandler

SECTION "FrameVars", WRAM0
wVBlank: DS 1                   ; set by the handler, cleared by wait_frame
wTicks:  DS 1                   ; frames elapsed

SECTION "FrameRoutines", ROM0

frame_init:
    XOR A
    LD [wVBlank], A
    LD [wTicks], A
    LDH [rIF], A
    LD A, IEF_VBLANK
    LDH [rIE], A
    RET

VBlankHandler:
    PUSH AF
    LD A, 1
    LD [wVBlank], A
    LD A, [wTicks]
    INC A
    LD [wTicks], A
    POP AF
    RETI

; HALT stops the CPU until an interrupt arrives, which saves power and makes the timing exact.
wait_frame:
.sleep:
    HALT
    NOP
    LD A, [wVBlank]
    OR A
    JR Z, .sleep
    XOR A
    LD [wVBlank], A
    RET

; --- end of frame.asm

When the interrupt arrives the processor stops what it is doing, saves its place, and runs the handler at $40, which sets a flag and counts. wait_frame does nothing but wait for the flag. The program turns this on with three things: frame_init enables the interrupt in rIE, the LCD must be on (without a picture there are no frames), and the program executes EI to allow interrupts at all. The HALT instruction is the important one: it stops the processor completely until the next interrupt, so the program does no work, wastes no power, and wakes at exactly the start of the frame. The NOP after it is a habit: on the SM83 the instruction after a HALT can misbehave in rare circumstances (the "halt bug"), so a harmless one goes there.

A program that plays music is, from this point of view, one loop:

loop:
    wait_frame        sleep until the frame begins
    ...               do this frame's work: check the buttons, move things,
    music_tick        update the music
    jump loop

Tempo in frames

If a beat is n frames, the tempo is

beats per minute = 59.73 * 60 / n

Thirty frames to a beat is 119.5 beats a minute, near enough 120. Twenty-four frames, the quarter note used by the song in Chapter 10, is 149 a minute. The usual lengths for a song are written in notes.inc as constants: N16 is 6 frames, N8 12, N4 24, N2 48 and N1 96, so that a bar is exactly 96 frames.

The whole frame is the finest division of time there is, which means a tempo is a whole number of frames. A real 140 beats a minute would be 25.6 frames; the program would have to play some beats at 25 and some at 26. The usual trick is a counter that adds the fraction each beat and carries into the whole number when it overflows, much as the wCount of the metronome below counts to its limit. Many games simply pick the nearest whole number, and the listener never knows.

A complete example

This metronome plays one click on every 30th frame, a high one on the first beat of each bar of four, and prints the beat number. Nothing else is happening, so it is the clearest picture of the loop. The click is a short square note whose length (Chapter 5) stops it before the next:

; timing_demo.asm - Chapter 9: keeping time with frames
; A metronome.  One beat is 30 frames, so (at 60 frames a second) the tempo is 120 beats a
; minute.  Every fourth beat is the high note.  The program does nothing but count frames.
DEF BEAT_FRAMES EQU 30

SECTION "TimingVars", WRAM0
wCount: DS 1                    ; frames since the last beat
wBeat:  DS 1                    ; which beat of the bar: 0 to 3

INCLUDE "boot.inc"
INCLUDE "notes.inc"

    CALL sound_init
    LD B, 2
    LD C, 2
    LD HL, Title
    CALL show_text
    LD B, 2
    LD C, 5
    LD HL, BeatLabel
    CALL show_text
    XOR A
    LD [wCount], A
    LD [wBeat], A
    CALL frame_init
    EI

MainLoop:
    CALL wait_frame             ; exactly one frame per time round the loop
    LD A, [wCount]
    OR A
    CALL Z, beat                ; the first frame of every beat
    LD A, [wCount]
    INC A
    CP BEAT_FRAMES
    JR NZ, .keep
    XOR A                       ; 30 frames have passed: back to the start of a beat
.keep:

    JR MainLoop

    LD B, 8                     ; show the beat number

    OR A

    JR NZ, .note
    LD A, A5
.note:

What you hear: a steady click, a higher one every fourth beat. The tests record the sound and find the start of every click: they are thirty frames apart, to within half a frame, for the whole recording, the accented beats are an octave above the others (880 and 440 Hz), and the frame counter wTicks goes up by exactly the number of frames that have passed.

Key fact: the screen is the Game Boy's clock. Sleep until the frame with wait_frame, count frames to measure time, and let the sound hardware keep the note going in between.

10. A Music Engine

[Back to Contents]

In High-Level Languages

Music is a file: MIDI, or tracker module, or a recording. A player library reads it, schedules every note, mixes the instruments and plays the result, and you call one function:

song = load("tune.mid")
song.play(loop = True)

Behind that call sits a sequencer, a program whose only job is to walk through a list of notes at the right moments and tell the instruments what to do.

In Game Boy Assembly

The Game Boy needs the same thing, and it is a small enough program to read in full. It is a sequencer for four channels, one stream of events per channel, driven by the frame clock of Chapter 9. Each event says what to play and for how many frames. Once per frame the engine takes one frame off each channel's wait, and when a wait reaches zero it reads the next event.

The shape of a song

A song is a header and four streams. The header holds the address of each channel's stream (0 means the channel is not used), then four instrument bytes: the value written to the envelope register of channels 1 and 2, the volume register of channel 3, and one more for channel 4 (which is not used: each drum carries its own). A stream is pairs of bytes: a note and a length in frames. Two special notes exist: REST (silence for the given time) and SONG_LOOP (go back to the start of this stream). On channel 4 the “note” is a drum number:

Song:
    DW LeadStream, ArpStream, BassStream, DrumStream
    DB $A2, $62, WAVE_FULL, 0         ; instruments

LeadStream:
    DB A4, N8, C5, N8, E5, N4, ...    ; note, length; note, length ...
    DB SONG_LOOP

The complete engine follows. The main entry points are music_play (set the header up), music_tick (one frame of music) and sfx_hold (keep the music off channel 1 for a moment):

; =============================================================
; music.asm - a small music engine (Chapter 10)
; =============================================================
; A song is four streams of events, one per channel.  Once per frame
; the engine counts each channel's wait down; when it reaches zero it
; reads the next event: a note (or rest) and how many frames it lasts.
;
;   music_play    HL = song: start playing it from the beginning
;   music_stop    silence the music
;   music_tick    call once per frame, straight after wait_frame
;   sfx_hold      A = frames: keep the music off channel 1 for a while (a sound effect is using it)
;
; A song begins with four stream addresses (channel 1 to 4; 0 = unused),
; followed by four instrument bytes: the value written to NRx2 for
; channels 1, 2 and 4, and to NR32 for channel 3.  Each stream is
; pairs of bytes:  note, frames.   A rest is REST; SONG_LOOP goes back
; to the start.  On channel 4 the "note" picks a drum from DrumTable.

DEF CHANNELS EQU 4

SECTION "MusicVars", WRAM0
wMusicOn:    DS 1
wSfxTimer:   DS 1                   ; frames left before the music may use channel 1 again
wDuration:   DS 1                   ; scratch
wChanWait:   DS CHANNELS            ; frames left in the current event, per channel
wChanPtr:    DS CHANNELS * 2        ; where each channel is reading
wChanStart:  DS CHANNELS * 2        ; where each channel began (for looping)
wChanInstr:  DS CHANNELS            ; the instrument byte of each channel

SECTION "MusicRoutines", ROM0

; --- Begin a song: copy the header into RAM and make every channel read its first event next tick ---
music_play:
    LD B, 0
.channel:
    LD A, [HL+]
    LD E, A
    LD A, [HL+]
    LD D, A                     ; DE = this channel's stream
    OR E
    JR NZ, .used
    LD DE, EmptyStream          ; an unused channel just rests
.used:
    PUSH HL
    LD A, B
    ADD A
    LD C, A                     ; C = 2 * channel
    LD HL, wChanPtr
    CALL .store
    LD HL, wChanStart
    CALL .store
    POP HL
    INC B
    LD A, B
    CP CHANNELS
    JR NZ, .channel
    LD B, 0
    LD DE, wChanInstr
.instr:
    LD A, [HL+]
    LD [DE], A
    INC DE
    INC B
    LD A, B
    CP CHANNELS
    JR NZ, .instr
    XOR A
    LD [wSfxTimer], A
    LD HL, wChanWait
    LD B, CHANNELS
.clear:
    LD [HL+], A
    DEC B
    JR NZ, .clear
    LD A, $40
    LDH [rNR11], A              ; channel 1 plays a 25% pulse...
    LD A, $80
    LDH [rNR21], A              ; ...and channel 2 a 50% pulse
    LD A, WAVE_ON
    LDH [rNR30], A
    LD A, 1
    LD [wMusicOn], A
    RET
.store:                         ; [HL + C] = DE (two bytes)
    LD A, L
    ADD C
    LD L, A
    JR NC, .nocarry
    INC H
.nocarry:
    LD A, E
    LD [HL+], A
    LD [HL], D
    RET

music_stop:
    XOR A
    LD [wMusicOn], A
    LDH [rNR12], A
    LDH [rNR22], A
    LDH [rNR32], A
    LDH [rNR42], A
    RET

sfx_hold:
    LD [wSfxTimer], A
    RET

; --- One frame of music ---
music_tick:
    LD A, [wMusicOn]
    OR A
    RET Z
    LD A, [wSfxTimer]
    OR A
    JR Z, .go
    DEC A
    LD [wSfxTimer], A
.go:
    LD B, 0
.channel:
    CALL channel_tick
    INC B
    LD A, B
    CP CHANNELS
    JR NZ, .channel
    RET

; --- One frame for channel B ---
channel_tick:
    LD HL, wChanWait
    LD E, B
    LD D, 0
    ADD HL, DE
    LD A, [HL]
    OR A
    JR Z, .fetch                ; nothing left to wait for: time for the next event
    DEC A
    LD [HL], A
    RET NZ                      ; still waiting
.fetch:
    CALL get_ptr                ; HL = the next byte of this channel's stream
    LD A, [HL+]
    CP SONG_LOOP
    JR NZ, .event
    CALL rewind                 ; end of the stream: start again from the top
    CALL get_ptr
    LD A, [HL+]
.event:
    LD C, A                     ; C = note (or REST)
    LD A, [HL+]
    LD [wDuration], A           ; the event's length in frames
    CALL put_ptr                ; remember where we have got to
    LD HL, wChanWait
    LD E, B
    LD D, 0
    ADD HL, DE
    LD A, [wDuration]
    LD [HL], A
    LD A, C
    CP REST
    JR Z, silence               ; a rest: stop whatever is sounding on this channel
    LD A, B
    OR A
    JR NZ, play_event
    LD A, [wSfxTimer]           ; channel 1 belongs to a sound effect right now
    OR A
    RET NZ
    XOR A
    LDH [rNR10], A              ; the effect may have left a sweep or another duty behind: put them back
    LD A, $40
    LDH [rNR11], A

; --- Start note C on channel B ---
play_event:
    LD A, B
    CP 3
    JR Z, .drum
    LD A, B
    CP 2
    LD A, C
    JR NZ, .lookup
    ADD 12                      ; the wave channel sounds an octave lower, so ask for one higher
.lookup:
    CALL note_freq              ; DE = pitch value
    CALL nrx2_address           ; C = this channel's NRx2 register (the note is done with)
    LD HL, wChanInstr
    LD A, B
    ADD L
    LD L, A
    JR NC, .nocarry
    INC H
.nocarry:
    LD A, [HL]                  ; the channel's instrument
    LDH [C], A                  ; NRx2 (or NR32)
    INC C
    LD A, E
    LDH [C], A                  ; pitch, low 8 bits
    INC C
    LD A, D
    OR NRX4_START
    LDH [C], A                  ; pitch high bits + start
    RET
.drum:
    LD A, C
    ADD A                       ; two bytes per drum
    LD E, A
    LD D, 0
    LD HL, DrumTable
    ADD HL, DE
    LD A, [HL+]
    LDH [rNR42], A              ; the drum's envelope
    LD A, [HL]
    LDH [rNR43], A              ; the drum's noise setting
    LD A, NRX4_START
    LDH [rNR44], A
    RET

; --- Silence channel B.  A playing channel reads its envelope register only when it is started, so
; the way to a quiet note without a click is to write volume 0 ($08: output still on) and start the
; channel again.  (Writing 0 would switch the output off, which can click on a real console.)
; Channel 3 has no envelope: its volume register is muted instead, and that takes effect at once. ---
silence:
    LD A, B
    OR A
    JR NZ, .go
    LD A, [wSfxTimer]           ; channel 1 may be playing a sound effect: leave it alone
    OR A
    RET NZ
.go:
    CALL nrx2_address           ; C = the low byte of this channel's NRx2 address
    LD A, B
    CP 2
    LD A, ENV_UP                ; volume 0, rising, no step time: silent, output still on
    JR NZ, .write
    XOR A                       ; channel 3: volume 0 (muted)
    LDH [C], A
    RET
.write:
    LDH [C], A
    INC C
    INC C                       ; NRx4: the start register is two on from NRx2
    LD A, NRX4_START
    LDH [C], A                  ; start again, at volume 0
    RET

; --- C = $12 + 5 * channel B: the NRx2 registers are $FF12, $FF17, $FF1C and $FF21 ---
nrx2_address:
    LD A, B
    ADD A
    ADD A
    ADD B
    ADD $12
    LD C, A
    RET

; --- HL = the stream pointer of channel B ---
get_ptr:
    LD A, B
    ADD A
    LD E, A
    LD D, 0
    LD HL, wChanPtr
    ADD HL, DE
    LD A, [HL+]
    LD H, [HL]
    LD L, A
    RET

; --- Store HL as the stream pointer of channel B ---
put_ptr:
    LD A, B
    ADD A
    LD E, A
    LD D, 0
    PUSH HL
    LD HL, wChanPtr
    ADD HL, DE
    POP DE
    LD A, E
    LD [HL+], A
    LD [HL], D
    RET

; --- Copy channel B's start pointer into its stream pointer ---
rewind:
    LD A, B
    ADD A
    LD E, A
    LD D, 0
    LD HL, wChanStart
    ADD HL, DE
    LD A, [HL+]
    LD C, A
    LD A, [HL]
    LD HL, wChanPtr
    ADD HL, DE
    LD [HL], C
    INC HL
    LD [HL], A
    RET

; --- Three drums: NR42 (envelope) and NR43 (noise setting) for each ---
DrumTable:
    DB $F2, $63                 ; 0: kick     - low, short boom
    DB $C1, $33                 ; 1: snare    - mid-pitched crack
    DB $71, $11                 ; 2: hi-hat   - high, very short tick

EmptyStream:
    DB REST, 255, SONG_LOOP

How it works

music_play copies the four stream addresses into RAM twice: once as where the channel is reading now (wChanPtr), once as where it began (wChanStart, for looping), and the four instrument bytes beside them. It zeroes every channel's wait, so that on the first tick each channel reads its first event at once, and gives channels 1 and 2 the duty cycles they use (25% and 50%).

music_tick loops over the four channels and runs channel_tick for each. That does three things. If the wait is not yet zero it takes one off and returns. If it is, it reads the next event: the note, then the length, stored as the new wait. And it plays the note. Reading the event is a pointer walk: get_ptr loads the channel's pointer, two bytes are read and put_ptr saves it. If the first byte is SONG_LOOP, rewind copies the start address back first.

play_event is the only part that touches the sound hardware, and it needs very little because the four channels' registers are so alike. The envelope register of channel c (0 to 3) is at $FF12 + 5 × c, and the pitch registers follow it, so one routine reaches all three square-or-wave channels: look up the pitch, write the instrument byte to the envelope register, then the two pitch bytes with the start bit. (Channel 3 plays an octave low, so the engine asks for a note twelve semitones higher, as in Chapter 7.) Channel 4 takes a drum from DrumTable: two bytes, the envelope and the noise setting of Chapter 8.

A rest calls silence, which writes $08 to the channel's envelope register (volume 0, output still on) and starts the channel again, as Chapter 4 explains, or sets the volume register of channel 3 to zero, so a steady note ends cleanly at the rest. An unused channel is given a stream that is one very long rest.

Where to call it

Here music_tick is called from the main loop, straight after wait_frame. Many drivers instead call it from the VBlank handler itself. Both are common. The main-loop way is simpler and keeps the handler short, but it has one weakness: if a frame's other work ever runs past the end of the frame, the tick is late and the music stutters. Calling it from the handler makes the music immune to that, at the price of running the whole engine with the interrupt busy, which must be finished before the next one is due. A game with heavy per-frame work should use the handler; this book's programs have none.

Sound effects over music

A game has both music and effects, and there are only four channels. The usual arrangement is that an effect borrows one: the music engine is told to leave channel 1 alone for a few frames, and the effect plays there. sfx_hold does exactly that, with a countdown in wSfxTimer that the engine checks before touching channel 1. The music carries on in the other three, and goes back to channel 1 at its next note, resetting the sweep and the duty cycle that the effect may have left behind.

The arpeggio and drum streams use the assembler's REPT to repeat the same pattern, so a bar of sixteen notes is written as four. REPT is a copy-and-paste done at assembly time and costs nothing when the program runs.

What you hear: a bouncing four-channel tune with drums that begins again after eight seconds, and a rising “boing” over the top of it when you press A, after which the lead comes back. Key fact: a music engine is a loop over the channels that counts frames and, when a count runs out, reads a note and writes the same few registers you used by hand in the earlier chapters.

11. Input & a Playable Instrument

[Back to Contents]

In High-Level Languages

Input arrives as events: onKeyDown(key) is called when a key goes down and the program plays a note in the handler. The operating system has already decoded the electrical signal into a key code and queued it.

on_key_down(key):
    synth.play(note_for[key])

In Game Boy Assembly

There is no event queue. There are eight buttons wired as a matrix, and one register, rP1 ($FF00), to read them. You tell the register which group of buttons to connect, the direction pad or the four buttons, and then read four bits back. A pressed button reads as 0, so the routine inverts what it reads. It does this twice, once per group, and joins the results into one byte, one bit per button:

; =============================================================
; joypad.asm - reading the buttons (Chapter 11)
; =============================================================
;   read_joypad   reads the pad; afterwards
;                   wJoy    = every button held now   (1 = pressed)
;                   wJoyNew = buttons that went down since the last call
;                 and A = wJoy.  Bits are the PADF_ values in hardware.inc,
;                 the same layout as the standard hardware.inc: buttons in the low nibble, direction pad in the high one.
; Call it once per frame, just after the frame begins.

SECTION "JoypadVars", WRAM0
wJoy:    DS 1
wJoyNew: DS 1

SECTION "JoypadRoutines", ROM0

; The eight buttons share four input lines.  Writing to rP1 chooses which
; group is connected: bit 4 low selects the direction pad, bit 5 low the
; four buttons.  A pressed button reads as 0, so the result is inverted.
read_joypad:
    LD A, %00100000         ; bit 5 high, bit 4 low: select the direction pad
    LDH [rP1], A
    LDH A, [rP1]
    LDH A, [rP1]            ; read a few times: the lines take a moment to settle
    LDH A, [rP1]
    CPL                     ; pressed = 1
    AND $0F
    SWAP A                  ; high nibble: Down Up Left Right
    LD B, A
    LD A, %00010000         ; select the buttons
    LDH [rP1], A
    LDH A, [rP1]
    LDH A, [rP1]
    LDH A, [rP1]
    CPL
    AND $0F                 ; low nibble: Start Select B A
    OR B
    LD B, A                 ; B = all eight buttons
    LD A, %00110000
    LDH [rP1], A            ; deselect both groups
    LD A, [wJoy]
    CPL
    AND B                   ; held now and not held before = newly pressed
    LD [wJoyNew], A
    LD A, B
    LD [wJoy], A
    RET

; --- end of joypad.asm

The routine keeps two bytes. wJoy is every button held right now. wJoyNew is only the buttons that went down since the previous call, found by inverting last frame's state and AND-ing it with this frame's: a bit survives only if the button is down now and was not down before. For a musical instrument that is the important one. Playing a note is something that should happen once per press: with wJoy a held key would restart the note sixty times a second and nothing but a buzz would come out.

The routine is meant to be called once per frame, straight after wait_frame, which is the pattern of Chapter 9. Every part of the program then agrees about which buttons were down on that frame, and the frame is long enough that the few milliseconds of contact bounce are mostly hidden.

A complete example

The eight buttons each play a note of the C major scale on channel 1, chosen so that the direction pad plays the first four notes and the buttons the rest: Right, Left, Up and Down give C, D, E and F; A, B, Select and Start give G, A, B and C an octave up. Finding the pressed button is a shift loop: shift the byte right until a 1 falls out, counting. The count is the bit number, and it indexes a table of notes written in bit order (A, B, Select, Start, Right, Left, Up, Down):

What you hear: a short plucked note for each button, as quickly as you can press them, and a different note for each. The tests press each of the eight buttons in turn and measure the recorded pitch: all eight are within 15 cents of the scale. They also check that the name printed on screen is right, and that holding a button down plays its note exactly once.

The loop is the one from the metronome of Chapter 9: one pass per frame, which means a note can start up to 16.7 ms after the button went down. That is below what a person can hear as lateness, and it is why the same loop works equally well for a game.

Conclusion

[Back to Contents]

Across eleven chapters one idea keeps coming back: describe the sound and let the hardware make it. A note is an 11-bit number and a start bit. A timbre is two bits of duty or sixteen bytes of wave. A fade is a byte in the envelope register, a sound effect's length is a counter in the channel, and a pitch slide is one more register. Even a drum is two bytes. What is left for the program is bookkeeping: count frames, and at the right moment write the next few registers.

What has and has not been verified

Every program here is assembled with no warnings and run on an emulated Game Boy (PyBoy) by an automated test, which records the sound the emulated chip produces and checks it against calculations done in Python: the pitch of each note, the duty cycle of each square wave, the loudness of each envelope, the length of each effect, the direction of each sweep, the shape of each wave and the clock of each drum. The note table is checked against the formula, the metronome against the frame rate, the music engine against the notes the song says, one channel at a time. The figures in this book are drawn from the same recordings, not from the textbook shapes.

Two things are worth knowing. First, nothing was run on real hardware, or heard through a real speaker. An emulator reproduces what the registers do, and the measurements above hold for it; a real console has an analogue stage, a small speaker and variations between models that no test here measures, and some sounds, such as the very high noise settings, will be filtered differently. Second, the tests can tell you that a pitch is 440 Hz, not that a tune is pleasing. Listen to the examples. The tuning, the envelopes and the song were chosen by ear, and the book's recommendations for particular sounds (a good kick, a good coin) are only that.

Some matters are deliberately left out: playing recorded speech or samples through the wave channel, the differences between the original console and the Game Boy Color and Advance in their handling of wave RAM and of the quirks of starting channels, and the details of the stereo and master volume stage. None is needed to write the programs in this book, and all are well documented elsewhere. The graphics of the console, with tiles, sprites and scrolling, are the subject of the companion book Pixels, Tiles & Sprites.
